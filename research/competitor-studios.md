## Content-strategy patterns observed

1. **Self-initiated flagship > everything.** DEMO festival (Dumbar), Design
   Threads (Porto Rocha), Flaneur (Yukiko's orbit), Lenis/open-source
   (darkroom), NDA podcast (Lucky Dip). Design press (Design Week, Sept 2025)
   documents this shift: studios become "cultural voices" — maker + curator.
   The platform attracts aligned clients and puts young studios on the radar
   of bigger ones.
2. **Founder/principal voice beats company page** at small scale. Personal
   accounts carry the voice; the company page reposts/links. (Snask, &Walsh;
   LinkedIn-for-studios analyses call this "principal-owned content".)
3. **Case studies as content, not portfolio drops.** Announce the work with a
   story (the strategic idea in one line: "From IRL to URL and back again" —
   Koto/Uniqode) and link to a deep case. Native carousel/document posts are
   LinkedIn's top format (~7% engagement per SocialInsider benchmark).
4. **Craft-philosophy posts for buyers, not designers.** Content that reaches
   founders/CMOs argues about process and business of design; Figma-tips
   content only reaches other designers.
5. **Cadence is modest.** Working small studios report ~6-12 posts/month
   mixing project spotlights, process, culture. Consistency > volume.
6. **Follower count ≠ prestige.** Yukiko ~1k vs Koto ~90k — both award-
   decorated. Reputation compounds through awards, press, jury seats, and
   objects/events that outlive posts.
7. **Vertical SEO** is underinvested across the category — e.g. "brand
   identity studio for X" queries have commercial intent and low competition.

## Deep dive — pricing, reviews, culture

### Pricing signals

Studios almost never publish prices; these are the real data points found:

| Studio | Pricing evidence |
|---|---|
| Studio Freight | **Publishes floors on its inquiry page**: smallest engagements ~$80k, comprehensive $250k+. Clutch profile: min project $250k+, $200-300/hr |
| Koto | Aggregators list $10k+ min, $100-149/hr — likely understated for their tier |
| Snask | "Inquire" only. Founder Fredrik Öst's published tactic: quote a high hourly rate, then negotiate on hours — clients accept more hours more easily than a higher rate |
| Industry benchmark (Clutch 2026) | Most branding projects $10k-50k; average ~$71.6k; typical US agency rate $100-149/hr; enterprise rebrands $100k-1M+; implementation adds 20-50% on top |

Pricing pattern: the stronger the positioning, the higher the floor and the
more public it can be. Freight's "$80k minimum" pre-qualifies clients and is
itself a positioning statement ("few select clients at a time... no quick
fixes"). Snask's published pricing column is content marketing too.

the Indian shops do) would be transparent-positioning in a market where
everyone hides numbers — and quoting in INR for local clients + USD for
international is the dual-rate model the data supports.

## Website critique — what the comps' own sites do right and wrong

Reviewed 2026-10-20 via live screenshots (research/shots/). Two patterns
matter most: the strongest sites put real work in the first viewport, and
every distinctive device doubles as brand. The weaknesses are almost always
the same ones — intro sequences that delay content, lazy-load gaps, and
nav-over-video readability.

### PORTO ROCHA — portorocha.com
- **Good**: fixed left column (wordmark + live clock + About card + project
  index) works as persistent orientation; right-side masonry mixes project
  imagery with news/press posts — awards and editorial features sit beside
  the work, so prestige compounds per scroll.
- **Bad**: dense and dark; the fixed column eats ~1/3 of the canvas; news
  cards and work cards compete for attention without clear hierarchy.

### Koto — koto.studio
- **Good**: minimal nav, yellow mark, live local clock detail; case studies
  carry real narrative depth ("18-month project, 15 markets, 50+
  sub-brands") — proof as copy, not just imagery. Nav collapses to a HOME
  pill on scroll — nice restraint.
- **Bad**: hero is clean but generic — a big image + "We're Koto" could be
  any agency; cookie banner interrupts.

### Studio Dumbar — studiodumbar.com
- **Good**: the site is essentially a showreel — full-bleed video of their
  work *in the real world* (VMAs on Times Square screens, DEMO festival).
  Work-in-context beats mockups. Almost no copy needed.
- **Bad**: almost no copy at all — zero positioning text above the fold;
  you have to already know who they are; heavy video assets gate the first
  impression.

### Studio Yukiko — y-u-k-i-k-o.com
- **Good**: custom condensed typeface is instantly recognizable; playful
  editorial greeting ("Welcome to design serenity. Please be sure to
  remove your shoes."); filter pills + clean project columns.
- **Bad**: scrolled sections can render as blank whitespace while lazy
  content loads — looks broken mid-scroll, exactly the kind of hole
  PerPixel's reserved-dimensions rule prevents.

### &Walsh — andwalsh.com
- **Good**: work-as-hero — full-bleed bold project video under a light nav;
  one-line descriptor + single "Our branding" CTA; uncluttered.
- **Bad**: nav text sits directly over bright video — readability depends on
  the frame underneath; minimal orientation for a first-time visitor.

### Snask — snask.com
- **Good**: voice is the design — "We are your future romance" over their
  own film; The Snask Feed gives every case a title + one-line description +
  GO TO CASE; shop link monetizes their merch/personality.
- **Bad**: heavy condensed type + dark video + dense nav competes with
  itself; personality risks overshadowing the work for corporate buyers.

### Otherway — otherway.com
- **Good**: memorable devices — mirrored wordmark, ticket-stub cards, the

  reads as a rendering bug until you understand it; asymmetric layouts leave
  awkward dead space at some breakpoints.

### OK-RM — ok-rm.co.uk
- **Good**: maximum restraint — a single serif statement on black

## What a "case study" actually is — and the anatomy the comps use

A case study is the write-up of one client project — the studio's proof
asset. It is NOT a gallery of final images; it's the story of the problem,
the thinking, and the result. Koto's Uniqode case is the template:

| Section | What it contains (Koto/Uniqode example) |
|---|---|
| Header | Client + one-line strategic idea ("From IRL to URL and back again") + industry tag (Fintech) + year |
| Challenge | Market context + what the client needed ("QR codes went niche → everywhere; cluttered brands; former Beaconstac needed an enterprise-ready identity") |
| Strategy | The ONE organizing idea the whole project hangs on ("bridge between physical and digital") |
| Identity | The system built: custom typeface, patterns, motifs — how the idea became form |
| Application | The work in real use: dashboards, QR scans, campaigns, sales decks — never mockup galleries |
| Outcome | What changed for the client ("definition and confidence to lead a crowded category") |

Why it matters mechanically: a case study is simultaneously (a) the sales
asset a referred lead reads before replying, (b) the LinkedIn post, (c) the
awards submission, and (d) the press pitch. One good project → four content
products. PerPixel's placeholder images currently do zero of these jobs.

## How studios actually get customers — channel evidence

Two independent datasets agree (JUST Creative's brand-industry survey; a
4,000-review Clutch analysis):

| Channel | Share of client discovery | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Referrals / word of mouth | ~34% (Clutch data); 86% of branders rely on it | THE channel. Past clients, alumni, adjacent agencies |
| Online search / SEO | ~21% | Vertical queries underinvested: "brand identity studio for [industry]" |
| Competitive pitch / RFP | ~7% | Clients shortlisting 2-3 studios |
| Previous relationships | ~6% | A client contact changes jobs → hires you at the new company |
| Review sites / directories | ~6% | Clutch, DesignRush — mid-market channel |
| Cold outreach | ~2.4% | Works, but weak as a primary channel |
| Portfolio / case studies | ~2.3% direct | But they're the conversion layer for ALL other channels |
| Social media | ~1.8% | LinkedIn/IG support, rarely close alone |
| Events / speaking | ~1.1% | Small volume, outsized prestige (Snask → Stanford, Google, SXSW) |

The real system, as the comps run it:

1. **Referrals are the engine** — everything else feeds or converts them.
   A referred lead still checks the site + cases before replying, so case
   studies are the conversion asset for every channel.
2. **Formal referral systems beat hope** — trigger points (ask at launch /
   positive review / quarterly check-in), a forwardable blurb, and
   partnerships with non-competing adjacent providers (dev shops,
   marketing consultants, PR firms) produce 2-4 qualified leads/month.
3. **Self-initiated platforms are referral multipliers** — DEMO festival
   (Dumbar), Off.Brand newsletter + Off.Live + Seasoned (Koto runs THREE
   content channels), NDA podcast (Lucky Dip: "it put us on the radar of
   bigger agencies"), InOtherWords (OK-RM), open-source tools (darkroom).
   Each creates discovery that referrals alone can't reach.
4. **Niche = shorter sales cycle** — Please See owns F&B/packaging;
   Thought Over Design owns consumer-brand strategy; being "the obvious
   choice for X" converts better than "full-service studio".
5. **Founder voice + speaking** — at small scale the founder's network IS
   the pipeline (Snask's lectures, Jessica Walsh's platform, Codesign's
   Dahiya on education panels).
6. **Awards/press are conversion aids, not lead sources** — they don't ring
   the phone, they close the lead who's already evaluating.

PerPixel implication: with zero named work yet, the fastest real pipeline
isn't social posting — it's (a) one strong self-initiated project that
demonstrates the range, (b) 2-3 adjacent partnerships (dev shops, marketing
consultants who need a design arm), (c) directory presence (Clutch +
Behance — free), and (d) each finished project turned into a Challenge→
Strategy→Identity→Application→Outcome case study so every future referral
converts higher.

## The pipeline in practice — stages, conversion, timing

Standard 6-stage agency new-business pipeline with benchmarks:

| Stage | Target duration | Exit criterion | Conversion to next stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intake | <24h | Lead logged + acknowledged, discovery booked | — |
| Qualification | <3 days | Fit confirmed (budget, authority, need, timeline) | interest→qualified ~40-50% |
| Discovery | 1-2 weeks | Problem, budget, decision process documented | qualified→pitch ~60-70% |
| Proposal | 1 week | Priced scope delivered | pitch→negotiation ~30-40% |
| Negotiation | 1-2 weeks | Terms agreed verbally | negotiation→close ~70-80% |
| Close | 1 week | Contract + deposit | overall win ~15-25% of qualified opps |

A closing proposal has 7 parts: the problem in the client's own words,
outcomes (not tasks), approach, named team, timeline, investment, and
2-3 case studies that MIRROR the client's problem — the last part is why
generic portfolios underperform case libraries.

Close rate by channel: referral inbound 32-45% · founder-content inbound
22-28% · SEO inbound 18-22% · tightly-targeted outbound 18-25% · generic
cold outbound 4-9%. Agencies with 30%+ close rates run documented
qualification before call #1 ends; meandering "discovery chats" close
12-18%. Pipeline coverage math: needed pipeline = revenue target / close
rate (e.g. ₹/£50k target at 25% → need 200k of qualified pipeline).

**Channel lead-times don't sync — stack them** (DemandDept timing data):
- Referral asks: 3-6 warm intros in ~30 days (needs past-client base)
- Cold outbound: first call wk 4-6, first close wk 8-12, predictable mo 4-5
- Founder content: first DM wk 4-8, inbound calls mo 3, flow by mo 6-9
- SEO: first lead mo 5-7, scaled mo 10-12
Top agencies run all four simultaneously; by month 12 the source stops
mattering. A referral-only studio that added structured outbound: 6,450
contacts → 61 meetings → 33 opps → 14 clients ($660k pipeline, $252k
closed, ~$20k avg deal, 42% close).

## Cold-start tactics — getting clients before you have clients

- **Spec work on real businesses** — redesign a real local brand in your
  target niche (menu/signage for a restaurant, label for a food brand,
  deck for a startup) without being hired. Labeled concept studies, they
  prove capability on the exact problem type you want to be bought for.
- **2-3 strategic discounted projects** — free/cheap ONLY for recipients
  who are well-connected, will actually implement the work, and will give
  a testimonial + referrals. Set terms before starting; it's an
  investment, not a habit.
- **Proximity beats platforms** — first clients come from people already
  around you: local businesses with weak sites, solo founders, agency
  overflow work, friends-of-friends, early startups.
- **Specific cold outreach only** — "I noticed your signup asks too much
  upfront + one redesign screen" beats "I'm a designer looking for work".
- **VC/accelerator referrals** — seed-stage founders need branding at
  funding moments; investors refer studios into portfolios (PrettyClever
  runs on this).
- **Build a micro-product for peers** — a free tool/publication other
  designers value makes them recommend you; darkroom's Lenis and OK-RM's
  InOtherWords are the prestige version. This doubles as content.
- **Identity-buying trigger**: rebrands cluster around launches, funding
  rounds, and expansions — target companies at those moments.
